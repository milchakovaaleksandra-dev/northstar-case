NorthStar is a fictional IT company with 800 employees. In 2025 it kept growing, but almost twice as many people left. I built a four-page Power BI dashboard to find out who is leaving, why, and what the company could do about it.

01

Ask

What does the business need to know?

The company is growing, but leaders feel that more people are leaving. Before spending money on retention programs, they want facts. The dashboard answers four questions:

  • How did headcount, hires and turnover change from 2024 to 2025?
  • Who is leaving: by reason, tenure, grade, department and location?
  • Can pay explain the exits? Are raises and promotions linked to performance?
  • Do sick leave or time without a project push people to leave?

03

Process

Cleaning the data and building the model.

The raw file had a few traps that broke the model. Each one showed up as a wrong number, and each one was fixed at the source in Power Query.

Summary rows inside data

Some sheets had total rows at the bottom, like “minimum salary ever”. One of them added 1,190 to the monthly payroll. Removed by filtering rows without an employee ID.

Blank keys

A blank project_id and a text row in the review dates stopped relationships and date logic from working. Removed and typed correctly.

Wrong relationship direction

Two links were set as many-to-many. Rebuilt as one-to-many from lookup tables to fact tables.

Two dates, one calendar

Hires and exits both need the calendar. Hire date is the active link; exit date is an inactive link, switched on in measures with USERELATIONSHIP.

Key measures I wrote in DAX:

  • Headcount on any date: hired on or before the date and not yet left. One measure works on cards, slicers and monthly charts.
  • Turnover %: leavers divided by average headcount (start and end of period), split into voluntary and involuntary.
  • Retention %: share of people employed on 1 January who are still here on 31 December.
  • First-year leavers, tenure at exit, salary on a date (average, median, quartiles), raise % from a “previous salary” column, bench % from active project assignments.
04

Analyze

What the data says, and what it does not.

Growth

The company grew, but turnover almost doubled

Headcount rose from 523 to 602 with 214 hires. But 135 people left, and turnover went from 13.4% to 24.0%. Headcount peaked at 624 in June 2025 and then fell for the first time.

Who leaves

People leave by choice, and many leave early

Four out of five exits are voluntary. Voluntary turnover rose from 10.8% to 19.2%. In 2025, 44% of leavers had worked less than a year, and 36 left in their first six months. Retention of people already on board fell from 88.8% to 82.6%.

So the problem is not only hiring more people, it is keeping new people.

Hot spots

The calmest places became the worst

Corporate Services had the lowest turnover in 2024 (7.4%) and the highest in 2025 (26.9%). Office B went from 8.1% to 25.3%. Comparing years made this visible; one year alone would hide it.

Pay

Pay level is fine, but pay is not linked to results

Average salary grew 12% and 83% of year-end staff got a raise, with two review cycles in 2025. Leavers earned the same as stayers in the same grade. But the regular raise was about 8% for every score from 1 to 5. Promotions (+22%) went to 22% of people rated 1 and only 4% of people rated 5.

Strong performers see no difference in money or career. That is a classic reason good people leave.

Projects

One project closure explains the July spike

Project 01 ended on 1 June 2025. The share of staff without a project jumped to 14.4%. In July, 24 people left; 10 of them came from that project. In September most people on the bench got new projects.

Not confirmed

Sick leave and the bench do not explain the overall rise

Sick days per employee fell from 4.5 to 4.1, with no hot spot in any department or office. In 2024 the bench was even higher (14–16% for eight months), yet few people left. These hypotheses were tested and rejected.

06

Act

What I would recommend to NorthStar leaders.

Fix the first year

Review the spring 2025 hiring wave: hiring quality, onboarding and the first manager. Add 30-60-90 day check-ins and a buddy for every new hire.

Link pay and promotion to performance

Make raises depend on the review score and make the manager’s promotion recommendation count. Review grade pay ranges, which overlap a lot today.

Investigate the hot spots

Run stay interviews in Corporate Services and Office B. Build a 2025 timeline of manager changes and redundancies, and check for a “survivor effect” after layoffs.

Plan before a project ends

Start moving people to new work before a big project closes, not after. Watch the bench every month and act when it goes above 10%.

Collect better data

Add exit interview reasons, the direct manager and a short quarterly engagement survey. Today the data shows who leaves, but not always why.

Next step: check whether top performers leave more often. A first look at the December 2024 review shows that 26% of people rated 5 quit in 2025, against 11% of people rated 1–3. The group is small (35 people), so this needs a careful second look.
